## EIC Review Report

### Reviewer Identity
Editor-in-Chief, IEEE Robotics and Automation Letters; specialization in legged/wheeled robot control with interest in novel control architectures bridging classical and learning-based methods. Assessing this manuscript for journal fit, originality, significance, and structural coherence for the RA-L readership.

### Overall Recommendation
**Major Revision** (with the candid note that this manuscript, in its current form, would be a strong ICRA/IROS submission; acceptance at RA-L requires either hardware validation or a substantially deepened theoretical contribution).

### Confidence Score

### Summary Assessment
This manuscript presents the Anchored Conditionally-Gated Controller (ACC), a classical control architecture for a 10-DOF wheeled bipedal robot simulated in MuJoCo. The core contribution is a proximity-gated anchor mechanism: a position integral confined to a 15 cm neighborhood around the home position, enabled by an asymmetric exponential moving average (EMA) envelope follower with fast attack (30 ms) and slow release (1.5 s). This envelope distinguishes quiet stance from post-push ringdown, allowing sub-millimeter idle precision (0.73 mm CoM RMS) without sacrificing omnidirectional push survival (F_min = 80 N across 8 directions). Four LQR baselines all fail (100% fall rate), which the authors attribute to the PID servo action path bottleneck rather than controller structure. Qualitative feasibility demonstrations cover 100 cm drops and 20 cm curb adaptation. The paper is exceptionally honest about its limitations: simulation-only results, limited statistical power (N=3--5 for several conditions), fundamental latency sensitivity below 10 ms, and absence of hardware validation. The engineering is solid, the ablation study is well-structured, and the self-assessment of limitations is among the most candid I have seen. However, the contribution is primarily an engineering solution -- a cleverly tuned classical controller -- rather than a theoretical advance, and all evidence is confined to a single MuJoCo model.

### Strengths

1. **S1: Exceptionally honest self-assessment of limitations**: The paper enumerates 10 specific limitations (Section VI), acknowledges when statistical power is insufficient (N=3 binary outcomes, Clopper-Pearson CI [0.29, 1.0]), explicitly labels flight and terrain results as "qualitative feasibility demonstrations," and correctly notes that borderline significance (L2 to L3, p<0.05) does not survive Bonferroni correction. Evidence anchor: `text: §VI "With N=3 binary outcomes, Clopper--Pearson 95%CI for 3/3 survival is [0.29,1.0]. Increasing to N≥10 is recommended before journal submission."`

2. **S2: Well-structured factorial ablation**: The additive (L0 to L3) and subtractive (S0 to S5) ablation design isolates the marginal contribution of each component with paired statistical tests. This is a model for how classical control papers should evaluate component contributions. Evidence anchor: `table: Table IV (Push Ablation)`

3. **S3: The asymmetric envelope follower is genuinely novel**: Using a fast-attack/slow-release EMA to distinguish quiet stance from post-push ringdown is a clever temporal separation mechanism that has no direct analogue in standard anti-windup, DOB, or ADRC literature. The paper correctly identifies why symmetric EMA fails (bistable mid-band lock) and validates this through ablation. Evidence anchor: `text: §IV-C "a symmetric EMA is bistable---post-push oscillation holds the EMA in a middle band where the gate is half-open and the boost cannot collapse the cycle"`

4. **S4: Thorough LQR baseline comparison with controlled ablations**: Rather than a single LQR baseline, the paper evaluates four variants (PID-servo, integral-AW, direct-torque, coupled 6-state 3D) with a 46-configuration gain sweep. The finding that direct-torque output improves survival 35% (0.52s to 0.70s) but does not prevent the fall, and that the coupled 6-state LQR performs identically to the 4-state variant, provides evidence for the PID servo path bottleneck claim. Evidence anchor: `table: Table VI (LQR Baselines)`

5. **S5: Complete parameter disclosure and open-source code**: All 50+ gains are enumerated (Table III), the repository is public with a pinned commit hash, and ablation configs are version-controlled. This level of reproducibility infrastructure is uncommon in single-author simulation papers. Evidence anchor: `text: §Acknowledgment "Code and parameters: https://github.com/thuong180702/Wheeled-bipedal-robot-simulation (commit f92640c)"`

6. **S6: Clear architecture-to-failure-mode mapping**: Each gate was added after a measured failure mode was traced to a root cause (bistable envelope, global-I windup, parametric pumping, hard-leash oscillation, self-referential stiffness). This design narrative -- "here is what broke, here is the gate that fixed it" -- is pedagogically valuable and lends credibility to the architecture. Evidence anchor: `text: §VI "Each gate was added after a measured failure mode was traced to a root cause"`

### Weaknesses

1. **W1: All results are simulation-only with no hardware validation**: The entire evidence base rests on a single MuJoCo model. While the paper provides a thoughtful hardware degradation estimate (Section VI) projecting 1--3 mm from the simulator's 0.73 mm, and outlines a staged commissioning plan, no physical robot data exists. For IEEE RA-L, which publishes archival results expected to persist beyond a single conference cycle, simulation-only validation of a controller whose primary claim is real-world precision is insufficient.
   - **Severity**: Critical | **Evidence Anchor**: `absence: §V (Experimental Validation) and entire manuscript — expected hardware validation or physical robot results; checked §I (Introduction), §V (Experimental Validation), §VI (Discussion)` | **Confidence**: 5 -- this is a binary observable (hardware data present or absent), and as EIC I can confirm none exists.

2. **W2: The "conditionally-gated" framing over-promises relative to the theoretical contribution**: The paper positions ACC as introducing a new class of controller ("conditionally-gated control"), but the individual mechanisms are standard components: gain scheduling (well-known since the 1990s), state-dependent integral gating (conditional integration AW), and envelope detection (standard in process control). The novelty lies in the specific combination and the asymmetric EMA design choice, not in a new control-theoretic framework. The abstract's claim of a "unified architecture" with "five control components gated by physical conditions" reads as more general than what is delivered -- a single controller for one robot morphology in one simulator.
   - **Severity**: Major | **Evidence Anchor**: `text: §I "We present the Anchored Conditionally-Gated Controller (ACC), addressing both dilemmas through conditional activation" and §IV "Conditionally-gated denotes continuous smoothstep activation by physical conditions"` | **Confidence**: 4 -- the control-theory genealogy is well-established; the novelty is engineering combination, not theoretical framework.

3. **W3: Insufficient statistical power for key claims**: The push ablation uses N=5, giving a minimum detectable effect of ~4.5 N. The paper honestly acknowledges this, but the central claim -- that the full anchor configuration outperforms ablations -- rests on comparisons where several pairwise differences fall below this detection threshold (e.g., L1 to L2: +1.1 N, n.s.; S0 to S4: -1.1 N). The terrain evaluation uses N=3 (Clopper-Pearson 95% CI [0.29, 1.0] for 3/3), making the 100% survival claim statistically uninterpretable. Flight ledge results use N=4. While the 70x idle precision improvement and S5 collapse are unambiguous, several secondary claims lack statistical support.
   - **Severity**: Major | **Evidence Anchor**: `text: §V-B "With N=5, the minimum detectable effect is ~1.5x the within-cell std (~4.5 N). Differences <5 N cannot be distinguished from noise"` | **Confidence**: 5 -- the statistical power analysis is provided by the authors themselves and is mathematically correct.

4. **W4: Single morphology, single simulator -- no evidence of generality**: ACC is demonstrated exclusively on one 10-DOF wheeled biped model in MuJoCo. The paper acknowledges this as limitation (8): "Architectural generality across morphologies and simulators remains to be demonstrated." The transferability guidelines in Section V-C (identify ringdown period, set tau_release = 3-5 x T_r) are speculative -- they have not been tested on a second platform. For a journal paper claiming an "architecture," some evidence of portability is expected.
   - **Severity**: Major | **Evidence Anchor**: `absence: §V (Experimental Validation) — expected second morphology or simulator evaluation; checked §III (robot platform, single model described), §V (all experiments use same MuJoCo model), §VI (limitation 8)` | **Confidence**: 5 -- the manuscript explicitly confirms single-platform evaluation.

5. **W5: Fundamental latency sensitivity undermines the hardware readiness narrative**: The paper's own data shows a sharp bifurcation: F_max drops from ~95 N to ~45 N at any non-zero delay (10 ms or 30 ms). Idle RMS degrades 5.5x at 10 ms. The paper presents ACC as "hardware-aligned" and estimates ~4.5 ms computation time (45% of the 10 ms window), but real-world IMU+odometry velocity estimation typically adds 5--20 ms of latency. This means the controller, as designed, may operate in its degraded regime on actual hardware -- a gap the hardware degradation estimate (Section VI) does not quantify for latency specifically.
   - **Severity**: Major | **Evidence Anchor**: `table: Table VII (Robustness) -- F_max: 96 N at 0 ms vs. 47 N at 10 ms; idle RMS: 0.42 mm at 0 ms vs. 2.30 mm at 10 ms` | **Confidence**: 4 -- the latency sensitivity is clearly measured; the hardware latency estimate is plausible but unverified.

6. **W6: The RL motivation is stated but not evaluated**: The introduction frames ACC as a "precondition for RL on this platform" and the abstract mentions PPO, but no RL results are reported in the paper. The CLAUDE.md project documentation indicates residual RL training is planned but not completed. This creates a structural mismatch: the introduction motivates the work by RL's failure, but the paper evaluates only classical controllers. Either the RL motivation should be backgrounded, or preliminary RL-with-ACC-prior results should be included.
   - **Severity**: Minor | **Evidence Anchor**: `text: §I "ACC is thus a precondition for RL on this platform: a height-general structured prior that a residual policy can later augment"` | **Confidence**: 5 -- the CLAUDE.md and git status confirm no trained RL results exist.

7. **W7: The platform comparison table (Table I) mixes quantitative and qualitative claims asymmetrically**: ACC's entries report specific numbers (0.73 mm, 110 N, 100 cm drop) while competitor entries report capabilities qualitatively ("Omnidir. (WBC)", "Discrete jump"). This creates an impression of superior ACC capability that may reflect reporting disparity rather than actual performance difference. For instance, Ascento has demonstrated jumping over obstacles and omnidirectional balancing on hardware; reporting only "LQR" for balance and "Discrete jump" for height without quantitative comparators systematically disadvantages the baselines.
   - **Severity**: Minor | **Evidence Anchor**: `table: Table I (Platform Comparison)` | **Confidence**: 4 -- the asymmetry is visually apparent in the table structure.

### Detailed Comments

#### Journal Fit
The paper addresses a relevant topic for IEEE RA-L -- wheeled bipedal robot control -- and the readership would find the anchor mechanism interesting. However, RA-L expects archival results with sufficient validation for lasting reference value. The simulation-only nature, single-platform evaluation, and limited statistical power make this manuscript read more like a strong conference paper (ICRA/IROS) where preliminary results and clever engineering solutions are standard. The 6-page RA-L format is appropriate for the content density, but the validation depth does not meet the journal's expectation of completeness. **Recommendation**: This paper would be a strong ICRA or IROS submission as-is. For RA-L acceptance, at minimum: hardware validation on a physical platform, or a second morphology/simulator demonstrating architectural generality, or a substantially deepened theoretical analysis of the asymmetric EMA's stability properties.

#### Originality
The asymmetric envelope follower for distinguishing quiet stance from post-push ringdown is genuinely original in the wheeled biped context. The specific combination of proximity-gated integral, scheduled pitch stiffness, and gated damping boost is novel. However, the "conditionally-gated" framing claims more theoretical novelty than is warranted: gain scheduling, state-dependent integral gating, and envelope detection are individually well-established. The paper appropriately connects to prior work (Astrom and Hagglund's selector-based control, conditional integration AW) but does not fully establish why this combination constitutes a new controller class rather than an engineered solution for a specific platform.

#### Significance
If the anchor mechanism transfers to hardware and generalizes across morphologies, it would offer a practical tool for wheeled biped platforms requiring both precision and disturbance rejection. The significance is currently bounded by: (a) simulation-only validation, (b) single-platform evaluation, and (c) fundamental latency sensitivity that may preclude deployment on hardware with standard sensor pipelines. The paper's most significant contribution may be methodological -- the structured ablation design and honest limitation reporting set a standard that other classical control papers should emulate.

#### Structural Coherence
The paper is well-structured. The abstract accurately previews the contribution. The introduction clearly states the problem (stiffness-precision vs. push-survival trade-off, integral windup). Section IV (Unified ACC Formulation) provides a complete mathematical description. Section V (Experimental Validation) maps each scenario to the component it tests. The discussion (Section VI) is exceptionally thorough in enumerating limitations. There is one structural tension: the introduction frames the work as enabling future RL (a "precondition"), but no RL results appear. The conclusion mentions RL only in passing. Either the RL motivation should be moved to a brief "Future Work" paragraph, or the framing should center on what the paper actually demonstrates: a standalone classical controller.

#### Title and Abstract
The title "Anchored Conditionally-Gated Control for Wheeled Bipedal Balance and Disturbance Recovery" is descriptive and accurate. The abstract is dense with quantitative claims -- appropriate for RA-L -- but over-promises in two places: (a) "ACC delivers omnidirectional push recovery" without noting the simulation-only context until the limitations section, and (b) "Flight recovery and per-leg terrain adaptation are demonstrated as qualitative feasibility proofs" buries the N=3--10 sample sizes. Recommend adding "in simulation" to the abstract's first quantitative sentence.

#### Conclusion
The conclusion correctly restates the main results and limitations. It is refreshingly free of grandiose claims. The future work direction (hardware validation, extending to dynamic locomotion) is appropriate. The conclusion would benefit from a clearer statement of what the reader should take away: is ACC a specific controller for this robot, a design methodology for gated controllers, or a template for RL priors? Currently all three framings appear in different sections of the paper.

### Questions for Authors

1. The asymmetric EMA is the key enabling mechanism. Have you analyzed its stability properties formally? Specifically, under what conditions on tau_attack, tau_release, and the system's natural ringdown frequency does the envelope reliably converge to zero after a disturbance? A Lyapunov or contraction analysis would substantially strengthen the theoretical contribution.

2. The latency sensitivity (bifurcation at <10 ms) is the most concerning result for hardware deployment. Your hardware degradation estimate (Section VI) addresses encoder quantization, backlash, stiction, compliance, and IMU noise, but not latency. What is your estimated end-to-end control latency on the target hardware (including IMU read, state estimation, control computation, and torque output), and what fraction of that latency does the ~4.5 ms computation represent?

3. You frame ACC as a "precondition for RL" (introduction) and the project documentation (CLAUDE.md) describes a planned residual PPO architecture. Have you run any preliminary residual RL experiments with ACC as the base policy? Even negative or partial results would clarify whether ACC's structured prior actually improves RL sample efficiency or final performance.

4. The platform comparison table (Table I) uses qualitative descriptions for competitors and quantitative metrics for ACC. Can you provide quantitative comparators for at least one competitor platform (e.g., Ascento's push survival force from the literature, or DIABLO's idle precision if reported)? If such numbers are unavailable, the table should note this explicitly to avoid the appearance of asymmetric reporting.

5. The 46-configuration LQR gain sweep confirmed that the PID servo path is the bottleneck. Did you test the coupled 6-state LQR with direct torque output (bypassing the PID servo layer, analogous to the LQR-DT baseline)? This is listed as deferred work, but it is the most direct test of whether the bottleneck is the servo path or the LQR structure itself.
