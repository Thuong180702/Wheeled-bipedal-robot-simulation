### Strongest Counter-Argument

The paper's core narrative -- "ACC achieves sub-millimeter precision without sacrificing push survival" -- is statistically true but semantically deceptive. The full ablation table (Table IV) tells a more damning story: **ACC's push performance is not better than P-only; it is slightly worse. The anchor is a precision mechanism that happens to not destroy push recovery, not a mechanism that meaningfully improves it.**

Consider the L0 (P-only) baseline: F_min = 69.7 +/- 1.1 N, F_med = 121.8 +/- 2.5 N. ACC (S1): F_min = 83.1 +/- 0.8 N, F_med = 118.8 +/- 1.0 N. F_min improves by 13.4 N (19%), but F_med **degrades** by 3.0 N. The P-only controller, with zero integral action and zero position homing, already achieves 122 N median push survival. Adding position homing (L1, +5.5 N, p < 0.001), a global integral (L2, +1.1 N, n.s.), and finally the full anchor (L3, +6.8 N, p < 0.001) cumulatively adds only 13.4 N to F_min while introducing a ~53x precision improvement. The ratio is instructive: a 19% push improvement for a 5,300% precision improvement. ACC's contribution is overwhelmingly about idle precision -- not about push recovery. The paper's title and abstract foreground "disturbance recovery" as a co-equal achievement, but the data shows ACC delivers essentially P-only push performance with radically better idle precision.

This reframing matters because it changes what the paper is claiming to have solved. If ACC's push survival is fundamentally bounded by the PD balance core (which is identical to the P-only baseline's core), then ACC has not "solved" the precision-push trade-off -- it has demonstrated that the trade-off is more separable than the field assumed. The anchor adds precision without subtracting push; it does not add push. The paper should claim this explicitly rather than implying the anchor mechanism itself delivers push recovery. The back-cover deformation from 96 N (forward only) to 83 N (omnidirectional F_min) -- a 14% drop -- further supports that direction-specific weaknesses are not resolved by the anchor.

A skeptical reviewer will ask: if a P-only controller achieves F_med = 122 N and F_min = 70 N, and a global integral controller (L2) achieves F_med = 133 N and F_min = 76 N but windups, then what exactly does the anchor add to push survival that couldn't be achieved by a simpler conditional-integration scheme? The additive path shows the anchor bundle contributes +6.8 N over global integral -- but this is below the minimum detectable effect (~4.5 N) and the fine-grained subtractive decompositions (S2, S3, S4 vs S1) show differences of -1.1 N to +3.8 N, none statistically significant. The anchor's push contribution may be measurement noise.

### Ignored Alternative Explanations/Paths

1. **ACC's precision advantage could be achieved by a simpler mechanism: a dead-zone + conditional integrator without the asymmetric envelope.** The paper compares against global I (which windups) but not against a simple position-dead-zone-gated integrator. The asymmetric envelope's primary role is enabling the damping boost for ringdown, not enabling the anchor integral for precision. If the anchor integral alone (gated only by g_prox, without g_env or g_boost) achieves comparable idle precision, then the asymmetric envelope is primarily a ringdown mechanism, not a precision mechanism. This decomposition is testable but not tested.

2. **The 0.73 mm precision may be a MuJoCo artifact, not a controller property.** The paper reports a noise floor of 0.002 mm CoM X RMS (SNR 289x). At 500 Hz simulation with semi-implicit Euler integration, the solver's numerical damping may artificially suppress high-frequency position drift that would appear on hardware. The paper's own projection of 1-3 mm on hardware (a 1.4-4.1x degradation) suggests the 0.73 mm figure is simulator-specific. If hardware achieves 3 mm, the paper's headline claim of "sub-millimeter precision" is invalidated. The paper should report the hardware-projected figure as the primary claim and treat the simulator figure as a lower bound.

3. **The LQR baselines' failure may be due to the specific LQR formulation (finite-horizon discrete-time LQR on a linearized TWIP model), not to LQR as a method class.** A continuous-time infinite-horizon LQR with integral action on the full nonlinear model (computed via iterative LQR/iLQR/DDP) might perform substantially better. The paper's claim that "four classical LQR baselines all fail" is true for the four specific formulations tested but says nothing about LQR variants not tested. The 46-configuration sweep validates the decoupled PD, not the coupled LQR's Q/R design space. A sweep over Q/R matrices for the 6-state coupled LQR -- varying the relative weight on pitch vs roll vs wheel velocity -- might find a stabilizing parameterization.

4. **The 10 ms delay bifurcation could be caused by the discrete-time implementation, not by a fundamental latency sensitivity.** At 100 Hz control, a 10 ms delay is exactly one control step. The paper uses a one-step delay (previous action held for one step) as the delay model. If the PD balance core has low phase margin at the Nyquist frequency (50 Hz), even one step of delay could cause instability. This is a known property of discrete-time PD control at low sampling rates, not a property specific to ACC. The paper should report the phase margin of the PD balance core at 50 Hz to distinguish ACC-specific latency sensitivity from general discrete-time PD limitations.

5. **The paper's claim that "no single classical controller integrates all three" (precision, push, terrain) may be true only because the field hasn't tried.** Industrial robots routinely achieve micron-level precision with disturbance rejection through high-gain PID + feedforward on stiff transmissions. The wheeled-biped field has focused on learning-based and optimization-based methods because they generalize to locomotion; the precision-push trade-off may simply not have been a priority. ACC may be the first to try rather than the first to succeed.

### Observations (Non-Defects)

- **The additive/subtractive factorial ablation structure is genuinely the strongest methodological feature of the paper.** The two-direction design (L0->L3 building up, S0->S5 stripping down) provides convergent validation that is rare in robotics. Even though N=5 limits statistical power for fine-grained comparisons, the STRUCTURE of the design enables conclusions that a one-direction ablation could not support. This design should be highlighted as a methodological contribution in its own right.

- **The S5 finding (pitch gate removal creates parametric oscillation) is the paper's single most scientifically interesting result.** It is not "component X is necessary for good performance" -- it is "removing component X creates a qualitatively DIFFERENT failure mode that is worse than having no system at all." This is the kind of emergent-behavior finding that justifies publishing a controller architecture paper. The paper should build its narrative around this finding more centrally.

- **The gate parameter sensitivity hierarchy (alpha_r 220.4, 20x the next parameter, 130x pitch gates) is a genuine practical contribution that partially addresses the "50+ hand-tuned gains" concern.** If only one parameter truly matters for porting to a new morphology, the tuning problem collapses from intractable to manageable. This finding should be elevated from Section V-E to a more prominent position -- it is, arguably, the paper's second most important result after the anchor mechanism itself.

- **The explicit disclosure that scheduled k_p provides zero benefit (S0 vs S1) and is excluded from the canonical specification is commendable.** Most papers would silently drop the negative result. The paper not only reports it but uses it to argue that fixed gains suffice, which strengthens the "simplicity" narrative. This is good science.

- **The staged commissioning plan and hardware degradation analysis are unusually thorough for a simulation-only paper.** Even though the 0.5 ms latency margin is optimistic, the mere existence of a margin analysis -- with specific numbers for each subsystem -- sets a standard that other simulation-only papers should follow.

### "So What?" Test

If ACC works exactly as claimed -- 0.73 mm idle precision, 80 N omnidirectional push survival, flight recovery from 1 m drops, and 20 cm curb straddling, all in simulation -- does it matter?

**For the wheeled-biped research community:** Yes, moderately. The precision-push trade-off is a genuine practical challenge. Demonstrating that a proximity-gated integral with asymmetric envelope detection resolves it on a 10-DOF platform provides a reusable design pattern. The 5-step porting procedure and sensitivity hierarchy (tau_release dominates) make the contribution actionable. The caveat is that no other lab has reported this trade-off as a priority problem -- the field has focused on locomotion, not precision standing. ACC may be solving a problem the field didn't know it had.

**For industrial practitioners:** Not yet. The 10 ms latency requirement, 0.5 ms margin, simulation-only validation, and absence of a hardware platform mean ACC is not deployable. The projected 1-3 mm hardware precision is good but not transformative -- industrial robots achieve this routinely with simpler controllers on stiffer transmissions. ACC's value proposition for industry is unclear.

**For the RL research community:** Marginally. The paper claims ACC is a precondition for residual RL but provides zero RL+ACC evidence. The pure PPO failure is a single negative result that does not establish necessity. Until residual RL experiments demonstrate that ACC improves sample efficiency, asymptotic performance, or sim-to-real transfer, the RL community has no reason to adopt ACC.

**For the control theory community:** No. The paper provides no stability analysis, no Lyapunov function, no describing-function analysis of the gate nonlinearity, and no formal guarantees. It is an empirical engineering contribution, not a theoretical advance.

**Bottom line:** ACC matters for a narrow audience -- researchers building torque-controlled wheeled bipeds who care about standing precision and push recovery, and who are willing to accept the latency and noise constraints. For the broader robotics community, ACC is an interesting engineering demonstration whose impact depends entirely on whether the precision claims survive hardware transfer.
